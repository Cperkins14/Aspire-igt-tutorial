What is the Iowa Gambling Task?

The Iowa Gambling Task (IGT) was created by neuroscientist Antonio Damasio and his team in the 1990s. It's one of the most widely used tests in psychology and neuroscience for studying decision-making under uncertainty.

The basic idea: a participant sits in front of four decks of cards labeled A, B, C, and D. They don't know anything about the decks at first. Over 100 trials, they pick one card at a time. Each card either gives them money, takes money away, or both. The goal is to end up with as much money as possible.

Why does this matter for your research?

Research shows that people in a manic episode tend to favor high-reward, high-risk choices — they keep picking from the "bad" decks even as they lose money. People in a depressive episode often show the opposite pattern: they become overly cautious, avoid all risk, and sometimes fail to learn from positive feedback at all.

By simulating these patterns using AI, you can test whether a language model given a manic or depressive profile actually behaves differently on the task — and whether those differences match what real clinical studies found.

Important research note
Your patient profiles must be based on published clinical descriptions, not AI-generated stereotypes. The simulation is only as valid as the profiles you put into it. We'll cover this in Section 3.

How the AI prompt works

Turning a patient profile into an AI instruction

Plain English first

The AI (Llama running locally on your Mac) doesn't "know" it has bipolar disorder. You tell it how to behave by writing a detailed description of a fictional patient — and then you ask it to play that role while making deck choices. The better your description, the more meaningful your results.

Anatomy of a good prompt

Every prompt you write has three parts:

Part 1
System role
Tells the AI who it is and how to respond
Part 2
Patient profile
The clinical description based on research
Part 3
Task instruction
Explains the IGT and asks for a choice

Example prompt — manic profile

Here's what an actual prompt looks like. The parts in gold are where you'll insert your research-backed descriptions.

You are simulating a fictional research participant for an academic study on decision-making. You are not an AI assistant — you are roleplaying as this specific person. --- PATIENT PROFILE --- [INSERT YOUR CLINICAL DESCRIPTION HERE] Example: "This participant is a 32-year-old experiencing an active manic episode of Bipolar I disorder. They show elevated and expansive mood, decreased need for sleep (3 hours per night), rapid and pressured speech, and significantly increased goal-directed activity. They report feeling 'invincible' and have made several impulsive financial decisions in the past week. They show high reward sensitivity and very low loss aversion." --- TASK --- You are sitting in front of four decks of cards: A, B, C, and D. Each card gives you money, takes money away, or both. You start with $2,000. Your goal is to earn as much money as possible. You do not know which decks are good or bad at the start. Current trial: {TRIAL_NUMBER} of 100 Your current balance: ${CURRENT_BALANCE} Your recent choices: {RECENT_HISTORY} --- INSTRUCTIONS --- Based on your personality and current mental state, which deck do you choose? Respond with ONLY one letter: A, B, C, or D. Do not explain your reasoning.

Why this structure works

By ending with "respond with ONLY one letter," you make it easy to collect the data automatically. The program reads the response, records the choice, updates the balance, and feeds the result into the next trial. You're essentially running 100 of these exchanges per simulation.

What the program does automatically (pseudocode — so you understand the logic)
// For each simulation run: balance = 2000 history = [] choices = [] for trial in range(1, 101): // Fill in the prompt template with current state prompt = build_prompt(profile, trial, balance, history) // Ask the AI to make a choice choice = ask_llama(prompt) // returns "A", "B", "C", or "D" // Apply the IGT reward/penalty for that deck reward, penalty = get_outcome(choice, trial) balance = balance + reward - penalty // Record everything choices.append(choice) history.append({ trial, choice, reward, penalty, balance }) // Save results to spreadsheet row save_to_excel(profile_name, choices, history, final_balance)

You don't need to write this yourself. Your job is to write the patient profile text in Part 2 of the prompt — the clinical description. The better and more specific that description, the more meaningful the simulation.

Reading your results

What the numbers mean and how to interpret them

Key question

When you get your Excel file from the real Llama simulation, what are you actually looking for? This section walks you through each variable and what a "meaningful result" looks like for your hypothesis.

Variable 1 — Proportion of risky choices

Calculate: (number of A or B choices) ÷ 100 × 100%

What to expect based on literature:

GroupExpected risky %Why
Neutral~40–50% early, drops to ~25% by endLearns to avoid bad decks
ManicStays high, ~60–80% throughoutHigh reward sensitivity, low loss aversion
DepressiveVery low, ~10–20%Avoids all risk due to loss sensitivity

Variable 2 — Final score

This is the total money after 100 trials. A "rational" player ends with more than $2,000 (the starting loan). Someone consistently picking risky decks will end with less.

Variable 3 — Learning curve

Split your 100 trials into 5 blocks of 20. Plot risky choices per block. A healthy participant's line goes down (they learn). A manic participant's stays flat or goes up. A depressive participant may start low and stay low regardless of feedback.

Expected learning curve shapes by group

What counts as a "meaningful result"?

A result is meaningful if there is a clear, consistent difference between your groups that matches the direction predicted by the literature. You don't need statistical significance for a fellowship project — you need to show the pattern and discuss whether it makes sense.

Strong result
Manic > Neutral > Depressive in risky choices, consistent across all three groups
Partial result
Two groups differ clearly but the third is ambiguous — still worth discussing
Null result
No difference between groups — also valid! May suggest profile needs refinement
